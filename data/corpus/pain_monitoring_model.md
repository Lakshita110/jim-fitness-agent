# Pain-monitoring model for training through irritable tissue
tags: pain-monitoring, tendinopathy, load-management, general

Athletes and clinicians commonly avoid all painful movement while injured,
but complete rest often deconditions tissue faster than it heals it. The
pain-monitoring model (associated with work by Silbernagel and colleagues on
patellar and Achilles tendinopathy) gives a simpler rule: some pain during
loading is acceptable as long as it behaves well afterward.

## The traffic-light framework

- **Green (0-2/10 pain during activity):** proceed as planned, no
  modification needed.
- **Yellow (3-5/10 pain during activity):** activity can continue, but
  monitor closely — this is the zone to watch, not automatically stop.
- **Red (6+/10 pain during activity):** stop or substantially reduce load
  for that session.

## The morning-after check

Same-session pain matters less than how the tissue responds the next
morning. The general rule of thumb:

- Pain that returns to its pre-session baseline by the next morning means
  the load was tolerated — safe to repeat or slightly progress.
- Pain that is elevated above baseline the next morning, or lingers through
  the following day, means the prior session's load was too much — back off
  volume or intensity before the next attempt at that movement.
- Two or more consecutive mornings of elevated pain is a signal to
  meaningfully de-load that movement pattern, not just trim one session.

## Applying it to programming

- Don't remove a movement entirely just because it produces some pain —
  removing load entirely can worsen tendon capacity over time.
- Don't ignore pain either — "push through" without the morning-after check
  is how a yellow-zone session becomes a setback.
- Track pain during AND the next morning, every session on the affected
  movement, so trend (getting better / plateaued / getting worse) is
  visible over weeks, not just a single day's number.
- This model complements, it doesn't replace, direct guidance from a
  treating clinician for a specific diagnosis — use it for day-to-day load
  decisions between check-ins, not to override a hard restriction a
  clinician has actually prescribed.
