# Patellofemoral pain: load-management guidance
tags: knee, patellofemoral, load-management, general

Patellofemoral pain (pain at/around the kneecap, often worse with squatting,
stairs, or prolonged sitting) is one of the most common knee complaints in
recreational and strength athletes. Clinical practice guidelines (e.g. the
JOSPT patellofemoral pain CPG) converge on a few consistent themes for
non-surgical management.

## What generally helps

- **Combined hip and knee strengthening**, not knee-isolated work alone —
  guidelines consistently favor programs that include hip abductor/external
  rotator strengthening (e.g. clamshells, side-lying hip abduction, banded
  lateral walks) alongside quad-focused work, over quad work in isolation.
- **Progressive quad strengthening** through a pain-tolerable range —
  closed-chain movements (squats, leg press, step-ups) in a range that stays
  in the green/yellow pain-monitoring zone, rather than full-range loaded
  work that spikes pain into the red zone.
- **Gradual exposure to the aggravating movement** rather than permanent
  avoidance — deep knee flexion under load (e.g. full-depth squats) is
  often reintroduced gradually as symptoms and capacity allow, not banned
  indefinitely.

## What to be cautious with

- High-rep or high-frequency deep-flexion loading (deep squats, lunges,
  jumping) early on, before the athlete has demonstrated they tolerate
  moderate-depth loading well over several sessions.
- Sudden increases in running or jumping volume — patellofemoral pain flares
  are frequently tied to a spike in load (a common ACWR-style
  overreach) rather than to any single movement being inherently unsafe.
- Prolonged positions of deep knee flexion (e.g. long runs, deep squat holds)
  when symptoms are in an active flare.

## Practical takeaways for programming

- Favor shallower range-of-motion or reduced load on the deepest-flexion
  variant of a movement when symptoms are elevated, and reintroduce range
  gradually as tolerance improves — a temporary substitution, not a
  permanent one, unless a clinician has said otherwise.
- Add hip-strengthening accessory work alongside any quad/knee-focused
  session rather than treating it as optional.
- Track weekly training load changes, not just the knee-specific exercises —
  a spike in unrelated volume (e.g. new running mileage) can be the actual
  driver of a flare-up.
