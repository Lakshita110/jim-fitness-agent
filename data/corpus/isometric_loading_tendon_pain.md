# Isometric loading for tendon pain
tags: tendinopathy, isometrics, knee, general

Isometric exercise (holding a static contraction against resistance, no
joint movement) is widely used as a first-line tool for irritable tendon
pain — most studied in patellar and Achilles tendinopathy (work associated
with Rio et al. and others), but the underlying logic generalizes to other
irritable tendon presentations.

## Why isometrics get used first

- Isometric contractions tend to reduce tendon pain acutely (an analgesic
  effect that can last hours), which is useful both for immediate symptom
  relief and to let heavier isotonic/dynamic training continue elsewhere in
  a program without the affected tendon derailing the whole session.
- Because there's no joint movement, isometrics can often be loaded fairly
  heavily (high percentage of a near-maximal effort) with less mechanical
  irritation than an equivalent dynamic movement, which is useful early when
  full-range dynamic loading isn't well tolerated yet.

## A general protocol shape

- Hold duration in the ~30-45 second range, for several reps (e.g. 4-5),
  at a joint angle that doesn't itself provoke pain.
- Effort level moderate-to-high (enough to be a real training stimulus, not
  a light hold) — but this should stay within the pain-monitoring model's
  green/yellow zone during the hold, and the tissue should still pass the
  next-morning check afterward.
- Isometrics are typically a bridge, not an endpoint — the general
  progression is isometric -> heavy slow resistance / isotonic -> more
  dynamic, energy-storage-type loading (e.g. jumping) as tolerance and
  capacity improve, not a permanent substitute for dynamic training.

## Practical takeaways for programming

- Isometric holds are a reasonable substitution for a dynamic movement that
  is currently in the red zone on the pain-monitoring model, rather than
  removing that muscle group's training entirely for the week.
- Don't treat isometrics as risk-free at any load — a hold that pushes pain
  into red, or that fails the next-morning check, should still be scaled
  back the same way a dynamic movement would be.
- Progress back toward the athlete's normal dynamic version of the movement
  as soon as it's tolerated, rather than staying on isometrics indefinitely
  once the acute flare has settled.
